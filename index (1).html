Spreadsheet sync. Drag an .xlsx/.csv here (or click to browse) to load a root dictionary that mirrors this exact grid — 5 rows for position, one column per syllable — plus a second "Compound Words" tab and a third "Secondary Meanings" tab (see below) in the same file. In Chrome or Edge, importing keeps a live handle to that file, so the button below writes your changes back into it in place instead of downloading a new copy every time; other browsers fall back to a plain download.
Every root syllable is plain consonant + vowel (13 consonants × 12 vowels = 156 syllables. The vowels are a i u e o, the separate vowels ë ü (short only — they can never be lengthened), and the long forms a- i- u- e- o-, written with a hyphen after the vowel so the vowel itself can carry a tone — the ledger's x‑axis). A root word is 1–5 of these syllables in sequence, and the same syllable means something different depending which position it sits in — that's the ledger's y‑axis. Position 1 is the head concept; positions 2–5 are modifier slots that narrow it. Example from the brief: si at position 1 = "fire", fu- at position 2 = "do" → sifu- = "burn". Click any cell to assign, reassign, or clear a gloss at that exact (position, syllable) address. Typing an unrecognised word in the builder below no longer creates a root silently — since that's just as often a typo as a new word — it asks first, and only allocates a random open cell in the relevant position's row if you confirm. The ↺ undo button in the top right reverses the last ledger or compound-word change if anything goes wrong. The grammar that follows the root is a single complex syllable — never a plain consonant+vowel shape a root could produce — so a word is always unambiguous about where the root ends and the grammar begins.
Position markers. The first five slots of the ledger (purple, before the consonant groups start) aren't ordinary meanings — they're the five bare vowels a i u e o with no consonant at all, and they only ever sit in position 1's row. Each one carries no lexical content of its own; it exists purely to tell the syllable that comes after it which position's table to read from (a→1, i→2, u→3, e→4, o→5), overriding whatever the natural 1st/2nd/3rd/4th/5th sequence would otherwise give you. They're for when strict sequential order doesn't work out — e.g. you want a modifier from position 4's table without building through positions 2 and 3 first. Use them from the "add next: marker" control in the phrase builder.
Secondary meanings. A root can carry extra meanings beyond its main gloss. Click a cell and fill in Secondary meanings (comma-separated) — the cell then shows a small +n badge and a tooltip. Secondary meanings are searchable in the ledger, appear in the thesaurus hints (as "secondary meaning of …"), and autocomplete in the builder; typing one at the right position resolves to the root that carries it instead of coining a new root. They live separately from the primary gloss, so they never use up a ledger slot or clash with another gloss. Clearing a root also clears its extras (undo restores them).

Compound Words

You can build a compound word right here — the same syllable-by-syllable picker as the phrase builder below, just not attached to a verb or noun yet — or you'll get one automatically the moment you give a multi-syllable root a whole-word label down there (like naming si+fu- "burn"). Either way it's tracked in this list so nothing lives invisibly. Compounds are never stored in this program — only in the spreadsheet — so import the spreadsheet each session to load them, and use "update"/"download" to keep any you add here (the page warns you before closing with unsaved compounds). This list lives in the same spreadsheet file as the root ledger above — a second tab named "Compound Words" — and updates in place the same way: import pulls both tabs in together, and "update"/"download" always writes the current version of both. A word's units are stored as plain position:gloss pairs (or marker:position for a redirect), so you can add or edit compounds directly in the spreadsheet too, as long as the syllable glosses they reference already exist on the Root Ledger tab. Plain CSV can only hold one table, so CSV import/export covers the root ledger only — compound words need the .xlsx form. Compound words can carry secondary meanings too: set them in the field above when saving, or with the meanings button on any row. Both kinds are stored on the "Secondary Meanings" tab (root rows are identified by position + syllable, compound rows by word; meanings are separated by ;), so they round-trip through the spreadsheet.

How grammar is marked

Every word has 8 grammar categories, written by four mechanisms. A category at its plain baseline is completely silent. Long vowels are written vowel + -, and a tone mark sits on the vowel before the hyphen (á-, à-); ë and ü are never long. Rising / falling are ´ / ` on a, i, u, e, o. The separate vowels ë and ü use the double marks instead, which replace the diaeresis: e̋ / ȅ for ë and ű / ȕ for ü.

1 · Tone on the root's first two syllables (position markers count as syllables). If syllable 2 is a marker vowel right after another vowel (as in kaawe), it just repeats syllable 1's tone, and the syllable-2 tone moves to the next vowel that isn't directly adjacent to another. Verbs: syllable 1 = aspect (rising imperfect, falling commencatory); syllable 2 = stative (rising restrictive, falling comparative; maximal and excessive go to the prefix). Nouns: syllable 1 = case (rising accusative / orig.noun, falling instrumental / pos.noun); syllable 2 = number (rising plural, falling none).

2 · Coda on the same two syllables (w l m x ʼ). Verbs: syllable 1 = evidentiality (none = empirical, w rational, l abductive, x inductive, m pragmatic, ʼ mystical); syllable 2 = negative + tense (w past, l past negative, x future, m future negative, ʼ negative). Nouns: syllable 1 = genitive (w inherent / accidental, l objectival / identitive, x owned, m nominative, ʼ composive); syllable 2 = negative / individuality / specificity (w negative, l negative + specific, x individual + specific, m individual, ʼ anything else).

One-syllable roots. If a syllable-2 feature is marked but the root has only one syllable, a position-marker vowel is put in front and becomes syllable 1: ni → ani. The marker is the one for the syllable's own position, so the root's meaning is untouched.

3 · Prefix consonants. Everything else is a syllable glued to the front of the word; its onset consonant names the option. Verbs: logic (position 1), reflexivity (2), habitual aspect (3), maximal / excessive stative (4). Nouns: logic (1). If an earlier position is silent, the syllable's coda jumps to its position (w→1, l→2, m→3, x→4).

4 · Prefix vowel pattern. The vowels of the prefix syllables carry mood (verbs) or position (nouns): 1st, 3rd… syllables take the first vowel of the pattern, 2nd, 4th… the second. a is the unmarked vowel, and patterns are handed out in order of markedness, so the commonest categories get the simplest patterns:
• one slot changed, core vowel — i–a u–a e–a o–a a–i a–u a–e a–o;
• one slot changed, peripheral vowel — ë–a ü–a a–ë a–ü (ë and ü are the rarest vowels, so they appear only after the core vowels are used up);
• both slots changed, harmonic pair (vowels that share backness or rounding, which is the easier thing to say) — i–e e–i u–o o–u ë–ü ü–ë;
• both slots changed, disharmonic pair — i–u u–i e–o o–e.
Verb moods use the first 11 patterns; noun positions use the first 16. If the prefix has too few syllables to show the pattern, bare vowels are added.

Vowel clusters. A word never has three vowels in a row. The third vowel of a run is palatalised — written with an inverted breve (a̯ e̯ i̯ o̯ u̯ ë̯ ü̯) and pronounced with a voiced fricative onset: a̯ e̯ = ɣa, ɣe; i̯ ë̯ = ʝi, ʝə; o̯ ü̯ = ʒo, ʒɰ; u̯ = ʝʷu. The onset starts a fresh group, so long runs come out two vowels at a time (V V V̯ V V̯ …), and it works across the prefix / root boundary.

Tone realization. On a short vowel, rising is simply a level high pitch and falling a level low pitch. Only on a long vowel (two moras) are they real contours: a rising or falling glide.
